Can an Elephant Talk to a Whale?

Margarita Samsonova · The Knysna Question · Field notes from a story two decades in the making

On the coast of Knysna, South Africa, in the summer of 2000, naturalist Lyall Watson believed he watched something extraordinary: a lone elephant matriarch — one of the last wild Knysna elephants on earth — standing at the water's edge, seemingly in communication with a blue whale beyond the surf. Watson chronicled the encounter in his 2003 book Elephantoms: Tracking the Elephant, describing what looked like a deliberate, reciprocal exchange between the largest land animal and the largest animal that has ever lived.

For most people, the story is a curiosity. For me, it is the beginning of a question I cannot let go of: if elephants and whales can reach each other, what else are we not listening to?

The legacy of Lyall Watson

Watson was no fringe figure. A South African biologist and author of the cult classic Supernature, he spent a career at the border of science and wonder — writing Whales of the World, studying animal behaviour on three continents, and insisting that the natural world is far more communicative than our textbooks admit. When he died in 2008, he left a quieter, larger legacy: a generation of researchers willing to ask what animals say to each other.

He was also right to be humble. Scientists who followed him have not confirmed an elephant-whale conversation — but they have kept the door open. The Elephant Listening Project at Cornell, founded by Katy Payne after her discovery of elephant infrasound, studies the low-frequency calls elephants use across kilometres of forest — calls humans simply cannot hear without instruments.

Whale songs for elephants

Inspired partly by Watson's Knysna account, German artist-researcher Tanja Boehme began playing whale songs to captive elephants in zoos across Berlin, Heidelberg and Leipzig in 2017-2019. The elephants stopped, raised a foot, tilted their heads — postures researchers read as deep attention. Her plan was to continue the experiment in the wild: playing elephant calls to humpback whales in Alaska with Cornell's Michelle Fournet, and listening near Gabon's coastline, where forest elephants meet the ocean. As the Elephant Listening Project reported, the point is not to prove a conversation — it is to take the animals' curiosity seriously.

“The clock is ticking, but by engaging hearts and minds, we can drive a global shift towards a more sustainable future.” — Margarita Samsonova, on why stories matter

Why this is my quest

My own work has circled whales for years — from my first sighting as a nineteen-year-old in Alaska to Whale Passage, the storytelling and research project I am building now. “Seeing them for the first time got me fascinated not just by whales themselves, but by how little we understand about their communication, behaviour and relationship with the ocean.” That sentence has quietly become the mission statement of my life.

The Knysna coastline is where elephants and whales share the same waters — the Knysna herd, the last free-roaming elephants of South Africa, and the southern right and humpback whales that migrate past the Garden Route every year. If any place on Earth would host an interspecies conversation, it is this stretch of Indian Ocean.

My goal: to retrace Watson's footsteps, film the Knysna shore, and bring whale and elephant researchers — and their recordings — to the same beach. Not to prove the impossible, but to listen properly, in the open, the way Watson did. And to tell the story in a way that makes the whole world lean in.

The Whale Meditation

Where the ocean becomes a teacher.

Margarita’s first whales were Alaska’s — spotted from a cruise-ship summer job when she was nineteen. “Seeing them for the first time got me fascinated not just by whales themselves, but by how little we understand about their communication, behaviour and relationship with the ocean,” she says.

That fascination became Whale Passage, her ongoing storytelling and research project, and it flows into her guided whale meditation sessions — quiet, immersive immersions themed around the ocean’s vastness, breath and sound, offered through “Voyage Across the Atlantic for the Whales” talks and Sacred Soul Fest collaborations.

No preachy eco-lecture. A stillness practice, tuned to the rhythm of the deep — leaving you calmer, and closer to the planet.
